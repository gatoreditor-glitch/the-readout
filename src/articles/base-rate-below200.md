Technicians call it the line in the sand. When the S&P 500 closes below its 200-day moving average for the first time in a long stretch, the commentary turns funereal: the long-term trend is broken, the bull market is over, sell everything. Since 2010 it's happened fifteen times. Six months later the market was higher 87% of the time, with a median gain of +13.2%.

## The numbers

- **Setup:** the S&P 500's first close below its 200-day average after an extended period above it.
- **What followed:** a median gain of **+13.2%** over the next 126 trading days (about six months).
- **Hit rate:** positive **87%** of the time -- 13 out of 15.
- **Sample:** 15 episodes, 2010 through 2026.

## What it means

The first break below the 200-day is one of the market's great contrary indicators. It *looks* like the end of the world because it usually happens amid genuine fear -- but that fear is precisely what creates the opportunity. Most first breaks are shakeouts, not regime changes: the market dips below the line, scares everyone, and recovers. This base rate is the statistical backbone of "buy the fear."

## The fine print

The key word is *first*. This setup measures the initial break after a long stretch above the average. Repeated breaks -- the market crisscrossing the 200-day for months -- are a different animal, characteristic of topping processes and real bears (2015–16, 2022). Don't apply this base rate to the third or fourth break. Also note what the 13% of failures look like: 2008-style, where the first break really was the start of something worse. The base rate favors buying the fear, but it demands a stop: if the market keeps falling and the break *sticks*, you're no longer in the 87%.

## How to use it

When the 200-day breaks for the first time and the crowd panics, let this base rate steady your hand: the historical playbook is to buy the dislocation, not join the liquidation. But pair it with a rule -- a further 5–7% decline from the break, or a decisive weekly close that fails to reclaim the line, flips the read from "shakeout" to "something bigger." The base rate gives you the courage to buy; the stop keeps you honest if you're wrong.
