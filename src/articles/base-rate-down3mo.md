Three consecutive down months is a special kind of misery. One bad month is noise; two is a concern; three is a narrative -- the financial press declares a new era, and capitulation starts to feel like wisdom. Since 2010 it's happened only five times. Every single time, the market was higher six months later. Median gain: +20.8%.

## The numbers

- **Setup:** the S&P 500 falls three calendar months in a row.
- **What followed:** a median gain of **+20.8%** over the next 126 trading days (about six months).
- **Hit rate:** positive **100%** of the time -- 5 out of 5.
- **Sample:** 5 episodes, 2010 through 2026.

## What it means

Three straight down months is the market's washout signature. By month three, weak hands have sold, pessimism is fully priced, and there is simply no one left to panic. What follows is snap-back: short covering, sideline cash redeploying, and the mechanical relief of a market that ran out of sellers. The +20.8% median six-month rebound is the largest short-horizon bounce in the database.

## The fine print

Five episodes is the smallest sample here, and 100% on five observations should be read as "hasn't failed yet," not "can't fail." These episodes also share a trait: all five occurred with policymakers able and willing to ease. Three straight down months *without* a policy response -- or with inflation tying policymakers' hands -- is outside the sample. Treat this base rate as describing washouts in the post-2010 policy regime, not a law of nature.

## How to use it

This setup is rare enough that you'll likely see it only a handful of times in an investing lifetime. When it fires, the base rate argues strongly against capitulation -- selling into month three has, historically, meant selling the bottom. The constructive use is to prepare a buy list during month two and three, when prices are distressed and sentiment is worst. Size positions for the possibility that the sample's perfect record breaks, but know the historical odds are overwhelmingly on the side of the rebound.
