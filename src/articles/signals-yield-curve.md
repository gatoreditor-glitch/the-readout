Before nearly every US recession of the modern era, the bond market sent the same telegram first: the yield curve inverted. Research from the New York Fed has shown the 10-year minus 2-year spread to be among the most reliable recession signals ever documented -- which is exactly why every inversion makes headlines.

The dashboard tracks that same spread, updated from Treasury data.

## What it measures

The 10-year Treasury yield minus the 2-year Treasury yield, in percentage points. Normally the number is positive: lenders demand extra compensation for locking money up longer, so long yields sit above short ones. When it goes negative -- short yields above long yields -- the curve is "inverted," meaning the bond market expects slower growth and rate cuts ahead. On this dashboard:

- **Bullish:** spread above +0.25pp. A normally sloped curve -- no warning.
- **Bearish:** spread below −0.25pp. Inverted -- the classic warning.
- **Neutral:** between the two. Flat, transitional, watchful.

## Why it works

The curve aggregates the expectations of the deepest market on earth. Banks borrow short and lend long, so a healthy spread means healthy bank profits and easy credit; an inverted curve squeezes that business model and tightens lending across the economy. It's a forecast made with real money, which is why economists take it more seriously than punditry.

## To be fair

The curve has predicted several recessions that never arrived -- and the miss can be spectacular. The curve inverted in 2022 and stayed inverted into 2024, one of the deepest and longest inversions on record, without producing a recession at all. Timing is also wildly variable: the gap between inversion and recession has ranged from a few months to two years. And here's the subtle one traders watch: the moment of maximum danger is often the *un*-inversion, when the curve normalizes because the Fed is cutting into a downturn already underway. An inverted curve is a warning. A normalizing one can be the all-clear or the trap door.

## How to use it

Read the curve as recession odds, not as a market-timing tool. A deeply inverted curve means the probability of an economic contraction within the next year or two is elevated -- so favor quality, keep some dry powder, and don't assume the cycle lasts forever. A normally sloped curve means no warning is flashing, which is valuable information in itself: most of the time, the correct read is "nothing to see here," and knowing that keeps you invested.
