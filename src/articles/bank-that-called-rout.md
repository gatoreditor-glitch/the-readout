Deutsche Bank downgraded tech on October 9, warning that risk-reward had turned unfavorable. Tech then fell 11% over three weeks while the rest of the S&P 500 gained almost 6%. On Saturday morning, the same bank upgraded global technology stocks back to overweight, arguing the selloff itself fixed the problem it flagged.

The bank that got the rout right is now saying the rout went far enough.

## The numbers behind the flip

The case is arithmetic, not enthusiasm. Mega-cap growth and technology underperformed the rest of the S&P 500 by more than 16 percentage points over three weeks, according to strategists led by Parag Thatte and chief strategist Binky Chadha. That slide pushed tech's relative performance to the bottom of its decade-long trend channel -- where prior rotations back into tech averaged a 32% gain, beating the rest of the market by 30 points, with the broader benchmark up 16% over the following three months.

Positioning did the other half: investor positioning in tech retreated from elevated to neutral. An emptied-out crowded trade is a different trade than the one Deutsche downgraded in October.

Friday's session was the exclamation point. The Nasdaq fell 2.86% to 25,437, its largest one-day percentage drop since June 5 and 7.8% below its all-time closing high of October 6. Intel plunged 28.6% on a weak outlook; Amazon fell 1.4% after reporting negative trailing free cash flow of $42.4 billion and holding its 2026 capex forecast at $220 billion. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index shed 7.2% in a day. Breadth was ugly: 3,622 Nasdaq decliners against 1,196 advancers, on volume of 19.87 billion shares versus a 17.26 billion average.

And here is the systems read: this dashboard's own gauges say the rout has not become a panic. Realized volatility sits at 11.3% -- up from 9.8%, but still calm. The S&P 500 proxy sits below its 50-day average but above its 200-day. A market that drops 2.9% in a day without volatility spiking is repricing, not breaking -- and Deutsche's upgrade is that observation dressed as a recommendation.

## To be fair: the bill that caused the rout has not been paid

Nothing in Friday's numbers resolved the question that started the selloff -- whether AI spending earns its keep. Amazon's $220 billion spending plan is intact. Intel's cash burn warning is intact. And the expectations Deutsche cites are still steep: consensus expects 36% earnings growth by the end of next year. If that gap closes by expectations falling rather than earnings rising, the "favorable risk-reward" reverts fast.

Deutsche names the tripwires itself: tech regulation after Tuesday's midterms, and further geopolitical escalation. It concedes further downside remains possible after another major shock. An upgrade at the bottom of a trend channel is a probabilities call, not a promise.

One more number keeps the upgrade honest. Even after the rout, tech and energy account for about three-quarters of expected third-quarter S&P 500 earnings growth of 31%. Strip them out and growth is still 12%, with the median company at 14.4% -- near a two-decade high outside recessions. The market's problem was never bad earnings. It paid for perfection, then got a bill for the infrastructure.

## Here's your plan

Do not buy the upgrade; test it. First, breadth: a durable recovery starts with decliners-to-advancers narrowing from Friday's 3-to-1 rout ratio, not one green Nasdaq day. Second, the channel: if tech's relative performance holds near the bottom of its decade trend channel, Deutsche's 32% average-gain history stays in play. Third, spending: the next round of AI capex plans either confirms $220 billion-style budgets come with returns attached, or it doesn't -- and that answer matters more than any bank's rating.

If all three point the same way, the rout becomes the entry Deutsche says it is. If breadth stays negative and spending plans keep swelling without returns, the bank that called the top of this rotation may have called its middle instead.
