Of the six gauges on the System Signals dashboard, five read neutral or better. One reads bearish: inflation, at 3.7% year over year.

Here's the five-year picture, built fresh from FRED data this morning:

![CPI inflation, year over year, last five years](/assets/charts/cpi-yoy.png)

## The numbers

- **September 2026: 3.7%** -- the latest reading, and the dashboard's lone red gauge.
- **June: 3.5%. March: 3.3%. Last August: 2.9%.** That's nearly a full percentage point of re-acceleration in twelve months.
- The Fed's target is 2%. We're sitting **1.7 points above it** -- and the gap has been widening, not closing.
- For context, the five-year range runs from 2.3% to 9.0%. We're nowhere near the 2022 peak. But the direction over the past year is unmistakable, and it's the wrong one.

## Why it matters more than the other gauges

Inflation is the binding constraint on everything else in the dashboard. Unemployment is 4.2% and flat -- the labor market isn't crying out for help. The yield curve is normally sloped at +0.33 points. Volatility is a calm 10.2%. Nothing else is flashing.

That's exactly the problem. With growth steady and inflation grinding higher, the Fed has no reason to cut -- and every reason not to. As long as this gauge reads bearish, the market's safety net stays folded up in the closet. Good economic news can't trigger rate-cut euphoria, and bad economic news lands without a policy cushion.

## To be fair

3.7% is less than half the 9% peak, and the economy has absorbed it so far -- unemployment hasn't budged in three months. Disinflation has stalled and restarted before; a hot stretch doesn't automatically become a new trend. But twelve months of grinding higher, from 2.9% to 3.7%, is more than a blip. It's a direction. And directions are what systems trade.

## Here's your plan

1. **Circle the next CPI release.** A print back toward 3.5% or below would ease the dashboard's one red gauge and reopen the rate-cut conversation. A print at or above 3.7% cements the re-acceleration trend -- and markets will reprice accordingly.
2. **Think in terms of Fed room, not Fed cuts.** With inflation re-accelerating, the right question isn't when cuts arrive. It's whether the Fed can cut at all if growth wobbles. Price your risk assets for a Fed with its hands tied.
3. **Respect duration.** Rising inflation is the enemy of long bonds first. And when rate-cut expectations get repriced lower, richly valued equities -- trading around 20x trailing earnings -- tend to feel it next.

One red gauge out of six doesn't make a bear market. But it's the gauge that decides whether the other five get any help if they turn.
