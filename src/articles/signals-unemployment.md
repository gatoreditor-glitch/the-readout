In mid-2007, the US unemployment rate sat at 4.4% -- a cycle low, the picture of health. Eighteen months later it was 7.3% and climbing toward 10%. The labor market didn't warn anyone. It confirmed, in its slow deliberate way, what the credit markets had been screaming about for a year.

That lag is the whole story of this gauge. Unemployment is the economy's slowest-moving vital sign -- and one of its most honest.

## What it measures

The U-3 unemployment rate -- the standard headline number -- plus its change over the last three months. The level tells you where the labor market stands; the change tells you where it's going. On this dashboard:

- **Bearish:** the rate rises 0.3 percentage points or more in three months. That pace of deterioration has historically marked the early stages of labor-market cracks.
- **Otherwise neutral-to-bullish:** steady or falling unemployment means households keep spending -- and consumer spending is roughly two-thirds of the US economy.

The 0.3-point rule is in the spirit of the Sahm Rule, the recession indicator economist Claudia Sahm built on a simple observation: once unemployment starts rising meaningfully from its low, it rarely stops on its own. Labor markets don't glide -- they tip.

## Why it works

Jobs are the transmission belt between Wall Street and Main Street. When layoffs rise, spending falls, corporate revenues follow, and more layoffs follow that. It's the feedback loop that turns slowdowns into recessions. A steady unemployment rate means the loop isn't running; a rising one means it may have started. No other single number captures the lived economy as directly.

## To be fair

This is the most lagging gauge on the dashboard, and it will never warn you in advance. Unemployment typically peaks *after* recessions end, not before they begin -- it confirms the diagnosis rather than making it. It can also sit at pristine cycle lows mere months before everything breaks, as it did through most of 2007. And the headline rate has blind spots: it misses discouraged workers who stopped looking and undercounts part-timers who want full-time work. Read it as confirmation, never as prediction.

## How to use it

Pair unemployment with the market gauges, not against them. When stocks are falling *and* unemployment is ticking up, the decline has economic sponsorship -- treat rallies as suspect. When stocks wobble but unemployment is flat and steady, the labor market is voting that the economy is fine, and history sides with the labor market more often than with a nervous tape. The gauge's greatest value is telling you which kind of selloff you're living through.
