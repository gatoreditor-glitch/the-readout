CPI is running at 3.7% year over year -- the September 2026 reading, fresh this morning. That's the bearish gauge on my dashboard, the single number keeping the Fed hawkish, and the reason rate cuts stay off the table.

But the level is only half the story. The chart is the other half.

![CPI year-over-year, last 5 years](/assets/charts/cpi-yoy.png)

## What the chart shows

This isn't a spike. It's a drift -- and drifts are harder to kill than spikes. One year ago, in August 2025, CPI was 2.9%. Six months ago it was 3.3%. Three months ago, 3.5%. Now 3.7%. Every check along the way points the same direction: up, slowly, steadily.

Zoom out to the full five-year frame and the shape tells the whole story. The 9.0% peak is ancient history now -- the chart's mountain, long behind us. The low was 2.3%, a whisper away from the Fed's target, and we left that behind too. We're not falling back toward it. We're walking away from it, one tenth of a point at a time.

That matters because the Fed's entire posture this year has rested on a single assumption: that inflation was settling. A settling number doesn't climb from 2.9% to 3.7% in twelve months. This chart is the evidence against the settling thesis, in one line.

## Why it won't just fade

To be fair, the rest of the dashboard refuses to panic. Unemployment is flat at 4.2% -- no labor-market distress feeding a wage-price spiral. The yield curve is a healthy +0.32 percentage points. Realized volatility is 9.6%, calm. If inflation were infecting everything, those gauges would be flashing too. They're not. So the honest read is: hot prices, calm everything else.

But look at this morning's wire for the pressure point: U.S. crude is holding above $100. Energy is the inflation component that moves fastest -- it can add tenths to CPI within a single quarter, and it doesn't wait for the labor market's permission. A 3.7% reading plus $100 oil is how a drift becomes a climb.

## Here's your plan

Pin this chart to your mental dashboard and watch two things. First, the next CPI print: does the drift continue toward 4%, or does it stall at 3.7%? The direction of the next tenth matters more than the level. Second, energy prices -- WTI is the benchmark to quote -- because that's the fastest transmission line from today's headlines into next month's inflation data. A drifting number is a patient number right up until the month it isn't.
