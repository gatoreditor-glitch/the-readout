CPI is running at 3.7% year over year. That number showed up on my dashboard this morning as the single bearish gauge out of six, and it's worth a closer look -- because the direction of travel is the story, not the level.

![CPI year-over-year, climbing from 2.9% to 3.7%](/assets/charts/cpi-yoy.png)

August 2025: 2.9%. March 2026: 3.3%. June 2026: 3.5%. September 2026: 3.7%.

That's a 0.8-percentage-point drift upward over thirteen months, in a straight line. Nobody declared inflation dead in so many words -- the market just priced like it was. SPY is at $769, a whisper above its 50-day line, in what the dashboard reads as calm, bullish conditions.

The market and the inflation data are telling two different stories. One of them is wrong.

## Why the drift matters more than the level

3.7% is not a crisis number. It's a policy number. It is the number that determines whether the Fed can cut rates into a market that has clearly already priced in cuts. Every 0.1-point step up in this series is another reason for the Fed to sit on its hands, and the series has been stepping up for over a year.

Here's the uncomfortable bit: the drift happened *while financial conditions were easing*. Stocks rallied, credit stayed open, the curve normalized to +0.38 points. Normally you'd expect easier conditions to eventually show up in prices -- and they did, just slowly enough that nobody had to update their story month to month. 0.1 here, 0.2 there. The frog-boiling version of an inflation scare.

To be fair, the counterpoint is real. Unemployment is flat at 4.2% with no sign of a wage-price spiral, and realized volatility is sitting at a sleepy 8.8%. An economy can run a 3.7% CPI with stable employment for a long time -- we did it for most of the 1980s and 90s, and stocks did fine. The dashboard's other five gauges aren't wrong; they're describing a genuinely stable economy. The question is only whether stable-at-3.7% gets to stay stable if the drift continues.

## Here's your plan

The October CPI print is now the highest-leverage data point on the calendar. Do the simple math on the possible outcomes:

- A 3.5% or lower print breaks the trend line of the drift. That would be the first genuinely good inflation news in over a year, and it would validate everything the market is pricing.
- A 3.7% repeat keeps the frog boiling. Markets can probably live with it -- they have been.
- A print starting with 4 ends the debate. The Fed stays hawkish, the curve reprices, and that 0.14% cushion above the SPY 50-day line gets tested immediately.

Until then, treat 3.7% as the number that decides what the other five gauges are worth. The trend, the calm, the curve -- they're all priced for an inflation problem that is, at best, not getting worse. The chart above says it is.
