The quietest gauge on The Readout's dashboard is doing the most reassuring work this morning. The yield curve reads +0.38 percentage points, flagged bullish. If you have ever wondered what that number means -- and why a fraction of a point earns a bullish label while inflation at 3.7% earns a bearish one -- this is the explainer.

## What the number is

The yield curve gauge is a subtraction: the yield on the 10-year Treasury bond minus the yield on the 2-year Treasury note. Right now that difference is +0.38 percentage points. The 10-year yields 0.38 points more than the 2-year.

That is the normal shape of things: lending for ten years should pay more than lending for two, because ten years carries more risk. A positive spread is the bond market saying the future looks roughly normal.

The gauge flips bearish when the spread goes negative -- an inversion, the 2-year yielding more than the 10-year. That is the bond market saying something is wrong: investors so nervous about the near term that they would rather lock money away for a decade than lend it for two years. An inverted curve has preceded every U.S. recession in the modern era, which is why the dashboard treats it as a warning light.

## Why +0.38 earns a bullish label

A spread of +0.38 percentage points is not a roaring endorsement. It is a normal, modest upward slope. But in the dashboard's logic, normal is bullish, because the alternative is the warning light. The curve is not saying the economy is booming. It is saying the bond market does not see a recession coming, and that absence of a warning is worth a green label.

That matters because of the company the curve keeps. Inflation is running 3.7%, hot enough to keep the Fed hawkish, and it is the dashboard's only bearish gauge. The split tells you the shape of the current problem: the bond market says the economy is not about to break, while the price data says the Fed cannot cut rates to help it along. No recession warning, and no rescue coming either -- which explains why the tape has gone nowhere for three months, three-month momentum at -4.9%.

## To be fair: the curve has cried wolf

The counterpoint is that the yield curve is a famously imperfect oracle. It spent most of 2022 through 2024 inverted, flashing its recession warning for two years, and no recession arrived. Anyone who sold stocks on it sat out a large part of the rally.

There is a second caveat. A positive spread is not always good news. The curve can steepen for the wrong reason: if long-term yields rise because investors fear future inflation, the spread widens while the news gets worse, not better. With CPI running at 3.7% and climbing from 2.9% a year ago, some of today's +0.38 points may be an inflation premium on the long end rather than a vote of confidence in growth. The spread alone cannot tell you which force is doing the widening.

## Here's your plan

Watch the spread the way you would watch a fuel gauge: not for the exact number, but for the direction and the crossings. If the spread drifts toward zero, pay attention -- the warning light is warming up. If it widens because the 2-year yield is falling, that usually means the market expects rate cuts, which is good for stocks. If it widens because the 10-year yield is climbing, check the inflation data first, because that is the bond market charging more for future price rises.

And read it alongside the inflation gauge, not instead of it. The curve answers one question -- is a recession coming? -- and right now its answer is no. Inflation answers a different one -- will the Fed help? -- and its answer is not yet. A market can live with the first answer for a long time. It is the second one that keeps it stuck.
