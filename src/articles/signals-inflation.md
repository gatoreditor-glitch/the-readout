Between early 2021 and mid-2022, US consumer price inflation ran from around 2% to 9% -- the fastest surge in forty years. It ended the era of free money, forced the fastest rate-hiking cycle in decades, and repriced nearly every asset on earth. Anyone who lived through it learned the lesson this gauge exists to teach: inflation decides what the Fed *can* do, and the Fed decides what markets can do.

## What it measures

The Consumer Price Index year-over-year change -- the broadest standard measure of what US consumers actually pay. On this dashboard:

- **Bullish:** under 2.5%. Near the Fed's target -- policy has room to ease if growth wobbles.
- **Bearish:** over 3.5%. Hot enough to keep the Fed on hold, or worse.
- **Neutral:** between 2.5% and 3.5%. Above target, but not alarming.

## Why it works -- and why it's bearish when hot

High inflation doesn't directly cause market crashes. It removes the safety net. In 2001 and 2008, the Fed cushioned market damage with aggressive rate cuts -- possible only because inflation was tame. With inflation running hot, that option disappears: cutting rates into rising prices risks an inflation spiral, so the Fed sits on its hands while markets fall. That's why this dashboard flags hot inflation as bearish. It's not the trigger. It's the reason there's no fire extinguisher.

## To be fair

CPI is deeply backward-looking -- it tells you what prices did over the last twelve months, not where they're going. Its shelter component, nearly a third of the index, lags real-time rents by a year or more, which means CPI can read hot long after housing inflation has cooled (and vice versa). One number also hides the breadth: 3.5% driven by energy looks very different from 3.5% spread across services. And the Fed watches its own preferred measure -- PCE inflation -- which usually runs a bit cooler than CPI. The gauge is a useful simplification, not the whole story.

## How to use it

Treat inflation as the binding constraint on the dashboard. When it reads bullish, the Fed has room to cut if anything else breaks -- good news for risk assets twice over. When it reads bearish, mentally downgrade every other bullish gauge a notch: the uptrend, the calm volatility, the steady labor market all have to survive *without* a policy rescue. Price your risk for a Fed with its hands tied, favor shorter duration, and remember that disinflation, when it comes, is the single most bullish development this gauge can deliver.
