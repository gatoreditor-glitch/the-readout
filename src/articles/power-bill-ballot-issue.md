Electricity prices never made the poster in American politics. This midterm, they are on it. Home electricity prices nationwide are up more than 10% since 2024, per Energy Information Administration data -- and in battleground states, bills have risen by triple the inflation rate. Ohio residential bills are up more than 20% over two years.

What changed is the explanation voters are being offered: the data center down the road.

## The $1.3 trillion question behind the bill

Follow the money. Utilities are on track to match last year's record $31 billion in rate-increase requests, per a PowerLines survey -- itself double the previous record. Investor-owned utilities are expected to spend a record nearly $1.3 trillion over the next five years, per S&P Global. Somebody has to pay for the grid feeding the AI buildout, and the default answer is the ratepayer.

That is why this election looks different. Seats on nine state utility commissions are up this year, including data-center hubs Arizona and Georgia, and governors will be chosen in 36 states. The promises followed the money. In Ohio, Democrat Amy Acton pledged lower bills, a commission "not beholden to utility companies," and a data-center moratorium; Republican Vivek Ramaswamy said Ohioans near data centers should pay no bills at all. In Pennsylvania, Governor Josh Shapiro, seeking reelection, cited PECO Energy withdrawing a rate hike in April, a move he said would cut future bills by as much as $34 a month.

There is already a winning playbook: New Jersey's Mikie Sherrill ran on freezing bills and did so in office, and Virginia's Abigail Spanberger, governing data-center-heavy Virginia, took a similar approach. Freezing a bill is popular. It is also a decision to move a cost, not remove it.

## To be fair: the grid does not run on the political calendar

The utilities have a case. Edison Electric Institute president Drew Maloney puts it bluntly: the grid needs steady annual investment and does not run on the political calendar. Advocates argue rate freezes and return cuts would not meaningfully lower household bills but could reduce long-term grid investment.

Fitch gave that argument weight in June, downgrading the North American utility sector to "deteriorating" from "neutral," citing political pressure to limit bills and profits. A downgrade is not a blackout prediction; it warns political risk is now a line item in the cost of capital -- and a higher cost of capital eventually lands in the same bills voters are angry about.

The systems read: the $1.3 trillion in planned spending is real demand for capital meeting a political movement to cap the return on it. One force gives way: either regulators let utilities earn enough and bills keep rising, or politicians cap bills and the buildout slows, shrinks, or gets financed another way. The campaign promises describe who pays. They do not change the size of the check.

## Here's your plan

Watch what happens after Tuesday, not on it. PowerLines executive director Charles Hua put it plainly: this could be the first midterm to feature electricity as a prominent issue, and "the real meat will come after the election." The check has three parts. First, rate cases: do new commissions approve, trim, or reject the $31 billion in pending requests? Second, data-center deals: do states charge large power users directly for grid upgrades, not residential bills? Third, utility credit: Fitch's "deteriorating" outlook says political risk is priced in -- if shares and credit hold up, the market bets the freeze promises soften in office.

Your own bill is the fourth check. If your utility's next rate case cites data-center load growth, that is the AI buildout's costs arriving by mail. Note the number and the justification, and compare both with what the candidates promised. That gap is the real story of this election -- and it will still be there in January, whatever Tuesday brings.
