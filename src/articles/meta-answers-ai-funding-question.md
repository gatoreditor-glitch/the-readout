On Sunday, the wire asked the question of the year: does enough capital exist to finance everyone's ambitious AI spending plans? On Monday morning, Meta answered. Not with a bond deal. With a $35 billion stock offering.

The sequence matters as much as the size. Meta launched the offering, the stock fell as investors weighed the dilution, and then it reversed those losses to rise as investors weighed the funding plan itself. That round trip -- down on the method, up on the meaning -- is the market thinking out loud about the single most important question in AI stocks right now: not how much gets spent, but who pays for it.

## Why the funding source is the story

Every AI spending announcement gets graded on the size of the number. Almost none gets graded on where the money comes from, and that is backwards. A dollar of AI spending funded with debt and a dollar funded with equity are not the same dollar. Debt has to be serviced whether the data centers pay off or not. Equity dilutes today's shareholders but adds no interest bill and no maturity wall. The risk moves from the balance sheet to the share count.

Meta chose the second kind of risk. A $35 billion equity raise says the company would rather share the buildout's cost with new shareholders than lever up to fund it alone. For a business generating enormous cash flow, that choice is a signal in itself: even the strongest balance sheets in this cycle are treating AI spending as too big to fund out of pocket without asking the market for help.

That reframes Sunday's capital question. The constraint on AI spending may not be whether capital exists. It may be how much dilution shareholders will tolerate before they stop applauding the spending and start pricing it.

## To be fair: equity is the honest way to fund a buildout

There is a case that Meta's route is the healthier one, and the stock's recovery hints the market sees it. A debt-funded AI boom leaves a trail of interest payments that survive even if the AI revenues disappoint. An equity-funded boom leaves a larger share count, but no creditor can call it. If the buildout stumbles, the equity-funded company bends. The debt-funded one can break.

The counterpoint cuts the other way too. Dilution is quiet. It does not show up as a scary headline the way a debt pile does, which makes it easier for management teams to keep raising and spending without ever facing a hard funding constraint. The discipline that debt imposes -- a lender saying no -- does not exist when the stock market keeps saying yes. Watch for that: the danger is not one $35 billion offering, it is the fifth one, when nobody remembers to ask what the spending is earning.

## Here's your plan

Use Meta's deal as the template for grading every AI funding announcement that follows it. Ask three questions before you applaud the spending number: is it funded with equity, debt, or cash flow; what does it do to the share count or the interest bill; and would you still like the deal if the AI revenues arrive two years later than promised?

Then watch whether peers copy the method. If more mega-caps follow Meta into equity offerings, the market has decided dilution is the price of staying in the AI race -- and investors who own the whole basket are paying that price in every name at once. If peers stick with debt or cash, Meta's deal reads less like a template and more like a company-specific choice. Either way, the funding line of the press release is now the most important line in it. Read it first.
