A 10% drop from the high has a name on Wall Street: a correction. It happens often enough to feel routine and hurts enough to feel like anything but. Since 2010, the S&P 500 has corrected fifteen separate times. Here's what happened next, every time.

## The numbers

- **Setup:** the S&P 500 closes 10% below its 52-week high (overlapping episodes counted once).
- **What followed:** a median gain of **+15.6%** over the next 12 months.
- **Hit rate:** positive **93%** of the time -- 14 out of 15 episodes.
- **Sample:** 15 episodes, 2010 through 2026.

## What it means

Corrections are the market's standard self-cleaning mechanism. A 10% decline shakes out weak hands, resets valuations a notch, and -- crucially -- has almost never marked the start of something worse. Fourteen of fifteen times, buyers twelve months later were sitting on double-digit gains. The base rate says the correction is the opportunity, not the warning.

## The fine print

Fifteen episodes is a respectable sample, but the one failure matters: 93% is not 100%, and the episode that failed was the kind where a correction metastasized into a real bear market. The base rate also can't tell you *which* correction you're in while you're in it -- every 20% bear market in history passed through −10% on the way down. And these are medians: the range of outcomes around that +15.6% is wide. Past episodes cluster in a long secular bull market; a different decade could write different numbers.

## How to use it

Use this as your prior, not your trigger. When the market is down 10% and every instinct says sell, the base rate says the odds favor buying -- or at minimum, not selling. Pair it with the trend regime gauge: a 10% correction that holds above the 200-day average is the textbook setup this base rate describes. One that slices through it deserves more caution, because you may be watching the 7% case develop in real time.
