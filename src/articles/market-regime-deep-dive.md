Six gauges. Three read bullish, two read neutral, one reads bearish. The S&P 500 sits at $763.25 -- below its 50-day average, above its 200-day, up 2.9% over the last quarter, moving at a sleepy 10.2% annualized volatility.

If you're looking for a clean narrative, this market won't give you one. What it gives you instead is something more useful: a precise description of a disagreement. The price gauges are cautious. The calm gauge is content. The economic gauges are mostly fine. And one red light -- inflation at 3.7% -- is quietly constraining everything else. Let's take the full readout, gauge by gauge, and then put it together.

## The price gauges are treading water

Start with [trend](/articles/signals-trend/): SPY below its 50-day moving average but above its 200-day. The short-term crowd has lost conviction; the long-term crowd hasn't. This is the definition of a mixed regime -- and mixed regimes are when markets do their chopping. Nobody has enough evidence to win the argument, so prices drift sideways and punish anyone betting big in either direction.

[Momentum](/articles/signals-momentum/) confirms the aimlessness: +2.9% over the last 63 trading days. Positive, but anemic -- the kind of quarter that describes a market going nowhere with conviction. There's no euphoria to fade and no panic to buy. Just... drift.

The base rates add useful context here. A market below its 50-day but above its 200-day is exactly the posture from which [first breaks below the 200-day](/articles/base-rate-below200/) have historically been buying opportunities -- 87% of the time, with a +13.2% median six-month gain. The trend isn't broken. It's undecided. Those are very different things, and the data says undecided markets resolve upward more often than the gloom suggests.

## The calm gauge says: no fear in the building

[Realized volatility](/articles/signals-volatility/) sits at 10.2% annualized over the last 20 trading days -- genuinely calm, comfortably under the dashboard's 14% bullish line. No forced selling, no panic, no euphoria. Just a market going about its business quietly.

Calm is constructive -- markets compound best when they're quiet -- but it's worth remembering what calm *isn't*. It isn't a forecast. It's a description of conditions. 2017 printed some of the lowest volatility readings in history right before February 2018 delivered a volatility explosion. The current calm means positions sized for quiet are appropriate. It doesn't mean the quiet is permanent.

What would change the read: a jump in 20-day realized vol from 10% toward 15% or higher. That's the market telling you the regime has shifted from drift to something with direction -- and direction, in either case, demands a reassessment of size.

## The economic gauges are giving the all-clear (mostly)

The [yield curve](/articles/signals-yield-curve/) -- 10-year minus 2-year Treasury yield -- stands at +0.33 percentage points. Normally sloped, no inversion, no recession telegram from the bond market. This is the single most reliable recession indicator in the economist's toolkit, and right now it's silent. Whatever is ailing the price gauges, it isn't the credit cycle.

[Unemployment](/articles/signals-unemployment/) agrees: 4.2%, flat over three months, nowhere near the 0.3-point deterioration that would trip the dashboard's warning. Consumers -- two-thirds of the economy -- are employed and spending. The real economy is fine.

Together these two gauges are doing something important: they're ruling things out. This isn't 2007 (no credit stress) and it isn't 2008 (no labor crack). Whatever the market's caution is about, the economic foundation underneath it is solid. Selloffs with this economic backdrop have historically been buying opportunities, not the start of something structural.

## The red gauge: inflation is the whole story

And then there's [inflation](/articles/signals-inflation/): 3.7% year over year, the dashboard's lone bearish read, up nearly a full point from a year ago and 1.7 points above the Fed's target.

Here's why this one gauge outweighs the others in importance even though it's outnumbered five to one: inflation is the binding constraint on policy. With growth steady (unemployment 4.2%), a normal curve (+0.33), and calm markets (10.2% vol), there's no economic case for rate cuts -- and with inflation at 3.7% and drifting the wrong way, there's no political room for them either. The Fed is boxed in.

That box is what turns an otherwise constructive dashboard into a capped one. In 2001 and 2008, rate cuts cushioned market damage. Today the safety net is folded up. Good economic news can't trigger rate-cut euphoria (the Fed won't cut into 3.7% inflation), and bad economic news lands without a policy cushion. The market's upside is capped by the Fed's inability to help, and its downside is unprotected for the same reason.

## The full read: constructive, capped, coiled

Put the six gauges together and the regime is: **constructive but capped, with stored energy.**

Constructive, because three gauges read bullish and the economic foundation (curve, labor) is genuinely healthy. The base rates agree -- new 52-week highs have led to gains 81% of the time, and the current posture resembles the setups that preceded solid forward returns, not drawdowns.

Capped, because 3.7% inflation keeps the Fed on the sidelines, removing both the upside accelerant (cuts) and the downside cushion. Until inflation bends back toward 3% or below, every rally fights a headwind and every decline fights alone.

Coiled, because 10.2% volatility plus +2.9% momentum plus a mixed trend is stored energy waiting for a catalyst. Big Tech earnings this week are the obvious candidate. The gauges describe a market holding its breath.

## To be fair

Gauges describe today; they don't promise tomorrow. The yield curve's clean record includes the 2022–2024 inversion that never produced a recession. Volatility's calm has preceded storms. Inflation's 3.7% could roll over next month -- disinflation has stalled and restarted before. And the base rates, for all their power, were computed in a 2010–2026 sample dominated by activist central banks; a regime where policymakers can't ride to the rescue is thinly represented in the data. Respect the read, but hold it loosely. The dashboard updates every morning precisely because regimes change.

## Here's your plan

1. **Play the regime you're in, not the one you want.** Mixed trend + calm vol + healthy economy + hot inflation = a market to be selective in, not to flee and not to chase. Favor quality, keep position sizes normal, and demand better entries than you would in a clean uptrend.
2. **Watch the two gauges that would change everything.** Inflation rolling back toward 3% would uncap the whole dashboard -- that's the single most bullish development available. Volatility spiking past 15% would end the coil -- in either direction -- and demand smaller size immediately.
3. **Let earnings be the catalyst, not your prediction.** Don't front-run the break. Define it in advance: a decisive reclaim of the 50-day average on strong volume says the uptrend is reasserting; a break of the 200-day says the coil released downward. Know your lines before the market tests them, and let the gauges -- not the headlines -- tell you which regime you're waking up in.

The market is arguing with itself. Your job isn't to settle the argument. It's to know exactly what would settle it -- and be ready either way.
