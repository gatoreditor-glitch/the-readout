A 3% down day is a punch in the stomach. The headlines scream, the portfolio bleeds, and buying feels insane. Since 2010 there have been fifteen such days (counted as distinct episodes). Three months later, the market was higher 67% of the time, with a median gain of +5.4%.

## The numbers

- **Setup:** the S&P 500 drops 3% or more in a single session (clustered episodes counted once).
- **What followed:** a median gain of **+5.4%** over the next 63 trading days (about three months).
- **Hit rate:** positive **67%** of the time -- 10 out of 15.
- **Sample:** 15 episodes, 2010 through 2026.

## What it means

Panic days modestly favor the dip-buyer. A −3% session is usually an emotional overreaction -- forced selling, headline-chasing, algorithms amplifying the move -- and emotions mean-revert faster than fundamentals. The market's tendency to stabilize and drift higher after the shock is real.

## The fine print

Be honest about this one: 67% over three months is barely above the market's everyday background odds. Stocks rise roughly two-thirds of all three-month periods *anyway* -- so this base rate is mostly telling you that panic days don't change the market's normal upward drift. And the +5.4% median is close to a normal quarter's return. The real value here is negative: it tells you what *not* to do. Panic-selling into a −3% day has no historical support. The edge to buying is small; the cost of selling is the thing to avoid.

## How to use it

Don't treat a −3% day as a screaming buy signal -- the data doesn't support that. Treat it as a *not-a-sell* signal. If you had a buy list, a panic day is a fine time to work it; if you were already fully invested, do nothing. The one thing the base rate clearly argues against is converting a one-day shock into a permanent portfolio decision. Sleep on it. The market usually does.
