In October 2008, the S&P 500's 20-day realized volatility ran above 80% on an annualized basis. For context, a calm market prints under 12%. That number wasn't a forecast -- it was a description of a market coming apart in real time, with 5% daily swings in both directions.

Realized volatility is the market's pulse: not what traders expect to happen, but what actually happened.

## What it measures

The annualized standard deviation of the S&P 500's daily returns over the last 20 trading days -- about one calendar month -- using the population standard deviation, since the gauge describes the full 20-day window rather than estimating from a sample. Annualizing just puts the number on the familiar yearly scale. On this dashboard:

- **Bullish:** under 14%. Genuinely calm conditions.
- **Bearish:** over 22%. Real stress -- the kind that forces selling.
- **Neutral:** between 14% and 22%. Elevated but orderly.

Note the framing: on this dashboard, calm reads as bullish. That's deliberate. Markets compound fastest in quiet conditions, and volatility clusters with drawdowns -- big down days travel with big up days, and the package deal is not one most investors enjoy.

## Why it works

Volatility describes the environment your positions have to survive in. Low-volatility regimes let trends develop and let compounding work; high-volatility regimes chew up leveraged players, trigger margin calls, and turn orderly declines into cascades. As a gauge, it answers the risk manager's question: how much turbulence is in the air right now?

## To be fair

This is the most backward-looking gauge on the dashboard, and the calmest readings often precede the storms. 2017 printed some of the lowest volatility in market history -- right before February 2018 delivered a volatility explosion that wiped out an entire class of short-vol products in days. Low realized volatility is a description of conditions, never a forecast of safety. Some of the sharpest selloffs in history began from exactly this kind of quiet.

## How to use it

Use volatility to size positions, not to pick direction. When the gauge reads calm, normal position sizes are appropriate and trends deserve patience. When it spikes past 22%, the regime has changed: reduce size, widen your mental stop distances (or you'll get shaken out of good positions by noise), and expect the unexpected. Professional desks do exactly this -- it's called volatility targeting -- and it's one of the few free lunches in risk management.
