Twenty percent down is the official boundary of a bear market -- the line where financial television switches to the red graphics and your stomach switches to something worse. Since 2010, the S&P 500 has crossed it five times. All five times, buyers one year later were up. The median gain: +24.2%.

## The numbers

- **Setup:** the S&P 500 closes 20% below its 52-week high (overlapping episodes counted once).
- **What followed:** a median gain of **+24.2%** over the next 12 months.
- **Hit rate:** positive **100%** of the time -- 5 out of 5.
- **Sample:** 5 episodes, 2010 through 2026.

## What it means

Bear markets are where generational returns are made, because they're the only time the market goes on a true clearance sale. A 20% decline prices in catastrophe; when catastrophe doesn't fully arrive, the snap-back is violent. This is the strongest base rate in the entire database -- the highest median return and a perfect hit rate.

## The fine print

Five episodes. Read that again: *five*. A 100% hit rate on five observations is suggestive, not conclusive -- flip a coin five times and you'll get all heads more often than intuition suggests. These five also share a common feature worth noting: every one of them ended with massive policy intervention (rate cuts, quantitative easing, fiscal bazookas). The base rate describes bear markets *in an era when policymakers always rode to the rescue*. If you ever live through a bear market where they don't, or can't -- say, with inflation running hot -- treat these numbers as the optimistic case, not the promise.

## How to use it

This is the base rate for the moment of maximum pessimism. When the market is down 20% and the consensus is that it's going lower, history says the expected 12-month return is roughly +24%. That doesn't mean buy with both hands on day one of the 20% print -- bear markets can always get cheaper first. It means your default posture at −20% should be accumulation, not liquidation. Scale in, respect that the sample is small, and keep some powder dry for the possibility that this time the rescue is late.
