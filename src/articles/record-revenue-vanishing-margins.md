Two companies in completely different businesses told the same story this morning. Procter & Gamble beat Q1 sales and profit expectations -- shares up 2% premarket -- while warning that higher commodity costs will take a bigger bite out of annual profit than it previously flagged. American Airlines posted record third-quarter revenue of $16.34 billion, ahead of the $16.22 billion consensus -- and forecast a deeper full-year 2026 adjusted loss of $1.00 to $1.60 per share because, per CNBC, record revenue couldn't offset this year's spike in fuel costs.

([Source: CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/22/american-airlines-aal-q3-2026-earnings.html) | [Source: Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/pg-flags-bigger-hit-to-annual-profit-from-higher-commodity-costs-beats-revenue-estimates-4965501))

Different aisles of the economy -- soap and shampoo, jet fuel and seat miles -- same enemy: input costs. That's the systems read, and it's the one story this earnings morning is actually telling.

## Where the bearish gauge shows up first

CPI is running at 3.7% year over year -- the lone bearish gauge on my dashboard, the reason the Fed stays hawkish. But inflation doesn't announce itself at the FOMC first. It shows up in earnings calls first, in the gap between revenue and profit.

That's exactly what today gave us. P&G sold plenty -- revenue beat. American flew plenty -- record revenue. Demand is intact in both stories. What's compressing is the margin between what comes in the door and what it costs to keep the door open. Commodity costs for P&G. Fuel for American.

This is the distinction that matters for the next three weeks of earnings season: revenue tells you about demand. Margin guidance tells you about pricing power. Two sectors with limited short-term pricing power against raw inputs both just told us the squeeze is real. That's a macro data point wearing an earnings costume.

## To be fair: the market noticed the beats too

P&G still rose 2% premarket. American still beat Q3 expectations on both lines -- an adjusted loss of 28 cents a share against 35 cents expected, plus the revenue record. The full-year forecast cut stings worse than the quarter, and here's the number to keep straight: July guidance ran from a 65-cent profit to a 65-cent loss. The new range -- $1.00 to $1.60 in loss per share -- is a loss on both ends. That's a real downgrade, not a rounding error.

But margin squeezes reverse. Input costs fall, hedges reset, fares get re-priced. The companies that come through these quarters with demand intact are the ones that snap back fastest when the cost side turns. Demand intact is the part worth underlining twice.

## Here's your plan

For every earnings report you read this week, skip the headline beat-or-miss and go straight to the margin language. Is the company raising prices, eating costs, or warning about the next quarter's input bill? Staples and airlines just showed you the template: revenue can be a record while profit guidance gets cut. The stocks to favor are the ones passing costs through without losing customers -- and the red flag is the company that stops talking about margins altogether.
