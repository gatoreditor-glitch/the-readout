## The systems angle

Through a systems lens, this is a factor-rotation signal, not just a commodity story. The dashboard's neutral trend gauge (SPY below its 50-day) and the slipping futures this morning show what oil spikes do to broad equities: they act as a tax on consumption and margins. Oil is, mechanically, a regressive tax -- it hits transportation, airlines, chemicals, and the consumer's wallet first, and shows up in earnings second.

That said, watch which damage is real and which is fear. A supply-shock premium -- which is what Mideast-tension headlines price in -- tends to be a spike on top of the price, not the trend itself. The trend is what took crude from $57 to $101, and that's a demand-plus-tight-supply story, not a headline one.

## To be fair

There are real counterpoints. Unemployment is steady at 4.2%, and GM just beat third-quarter earnings expectations -- consumer-facing demand is not collapsing under fuel prices. And a strong dollar-era oil market can absorb higher prices without the wage-price spiral that made the 1970s unbearable. Energy as a share of household budgets is far smaller than it was then.

But the margin of safety is thin. CPI already at 3.7% leaves no room for energy pass-through before the Fed's job gets harder. The last thing a neutral-trending market with 3.7% inflation needs is a fuel-cost shock.

## Here's your plan

1. Watch the next CPI print for the energy line item -- that's where a $100-plus crude print becomes a Fed problem.
2. Don't fight the rotation mechanically: in energy-led markets, capital flows toward producers and away from high-multiple consumers of energy. Let the price confirm the shift before you chase it.
3. Keep the 10-year/2-year curve (+0.35 pp, still healthy) on your radar. If the curve starts flattening while oil climbs, that's the market pricing in demand destruction -- and that's a different, uglier regime.
