They set the price of money for hundreds of millions of people, and their departures usually carry at least a whiff of uncertainty. ## Why leadership changes are lagging risks, not instant ones

Here is the systems read on transitions like this. The risk in a leadership change rarely lands on announcement day. It lands in the gap: the weeks or months between the known departure and the unknown successor, when every policy statement gets read twice -- once for what it says, and once for what it might mean under the next person. Markets are good at pricing known events. They are bad at pricing vacancies, because a vacancy is not a policy. It is a space where policy could go several directions at once.

February gives that gap a defined shape: a few months away, so the succession conversation has time to develop, wobble, and resolve. The questions that will move markets are not about the departure. They are about the successor: continuity or shift, orderly or contested, and whether ECB guidance language changes tone in the meantime?

That is also why the calm reaction should not be over-read. A market that rises on departure news is not endorsing the departure -- it is declining to panic about consequences that have not arrived yet. The departure is priced. The succession is not, because there is nothing to price yet.

## What to watch in the window

Three signals matter more than the next headline about the exit. First, the succession itself: the name, the background, and above all whether markets read the choice as continuity or change. A central banker with a known hawkish or dovish tilt reprices rate expectations on appointment day, before a single decision is made. Second, the ECB's language between now and February: steady guidance through a transition signals institutional depth, while sudden shifts invite speculation about who is really steering. Third, the euro's behavior around succession news rather than departure news -- currencies are where leadership expectations show up first.

None of this requires predicting the successor. It requires watching the process: orderly or messy, quick or drawn out, consensus or contested.

## To be fair: central banks are built for exactly this

The counterpoint is institutional, and it is strong. The ECB is not a one-person operation. It is a governing council, a staff of economists, and a mandate written into treaty. Presidents come and go; the institution's framework outlasts them, and markets know it. That is arguably why shares inched up: investors priced the institution's durability, not one leader's irreplaceability. Leadership transitions at major central banks are normal, procedural events, not crises, and treating a scheduled February departure as a shock would be a category error.

There is also a simpler reading of the calm: on a morning crowded with earnings -- Eli Lilly and Merck both beating and raising outlooks -- a February departure may have simply ranked below the day's more immediate news. Not every calm reaction is a deep signal.

## Here's your plan

File the departure, then watch the succession. The check is specific: when a successor is named, ask one question before reacting to the market's first move -- does this choice look like continuity with Lagarde's ECB, or a change of direction? If continuity, the transition is mostly noise and the calm reaction was right. If a shift, re-read every ECB statement from the window with fresh eyes, because the market will start repricing rate expectations before the new president takes a single vote. The exit is news. The entrance is the trade.
