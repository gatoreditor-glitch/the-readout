Every boom has two phases. In the first, everyone gets funded. In the second, the market starts asking who actually survives. The neocloud business -- the upstarts renting out AI computing power -- entered its second phase on Monday morning, and the tape did not bother to be subtle about it.

Elon Musk said more than 200 neocloud firms face what he called "guppy-level death," in a warning carried by CNBC. On the same morning, Investing.com was running separate explainers on why two of the best-known names in the space, CoreWeave and Nebius, were sliding. A warning about 200 firms plus same-day slides in the sector's headline names is not a coincidence to wave away. It is a sorting event, happening in real time.

## What a cull actually looks like

A cull does not announce itself with a crash. It looks like this: a respected voice says most of the field will not make it, a few prominent stocks fall on the same day, and the rest of the sector trades as if it is waiting to find out whether it is on the list. The mechanism is straightforward. Neoclouds rent expensive chips, finance them, and rent the computing power onward. That model works beautifully when demand outruns supply and customers take whatever capacity they can get. It gets brutal the moment capacity catches up, prices soften, or a customer decides to build in-house instead of renting.

The 200 number is the point, not the color of the language around it. If even a fraction of that many firms are fighting for the same customers with the same rented hardware, the survivors will be the ones with locked-in contracts, cheap financing, or a cost edge -- not the ones with the best pitch deck. Hardware can be rented by anyone. A durable customer base cannot.

## To be fair: a supplier shakeout is not an AI verdict

Here is the distinction that keeps this story honest. A shakeout among the companies renting out AI capacity is not the same thing as a collapse in demand for AI capacity. On the very same morning, Verizon lifted its annual forecasts and signed a deal worth more than $2 billion for AI infrastructure with AWS. The demand side of this market is still signing checks. The supply side is where the casualties are piling up.

That gap -- strong demand, fragile suppliers -- is the classic shape of an infrastructure buildout maturing. Railroads, telecom networks, and dot-com hosting all went through a version of it: the thing being built was real, and most of the companies building it still failed. Investors who confused "AI demand is real" with "every AI supplier wins" were making the same error in every one of those cycles.

## Here's your plan

Split your AI exposure in two in your head before you touch any position: the demand side and the supplier side. On the demand side, look for signed deals, recurring revenue, and customers with names you recognize -- Verizon's $2 billion AWS deal is the template. On the supplier side, demand proof of differentiation: contracted backlog that outlasts the hardware cycle, financing costs that do not eat the margin, and customers who cannot walk away cheaply.

And do not buy a neocloud dip on the thesis alone. A stock that falls because its sector is being culled is not automatically cheap; it may be correctly priced for a fight it loses. Wait for the company to show you which side of the sorting it lands on -- a major contract renewal, a financing deal on decent terms, a quarter where margins hold while a rival's do not. In a cull, the survivors announce themselves. Your job is to let them.
